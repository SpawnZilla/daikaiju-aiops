# Bounded Autonomy Is an Authorization Property, Not a Model Property: A Case Study of Daikaiju AIOps

**Research whitepaper · 25 November 2026 · Case-study revision: `SpawnZilla/daikaiju-aiops` at `2e5e3f1`, test evidence updated to `8179350`**

## Abstract

Can an AI agent operate independently without making its operator accept unbounded risk? This paper advances a conditional, falsifiable claim: **an agentic operations system can be authorized to act without per-action human approval within a narrow, independently enforced envelope, provided its actions are constrained to verified inputs, preauthorized effects, bounded retries, auditable outcomes, and promptly revocable privileges.** This is *bounded operational autonomy*, not proof that a language model is generally reliable or that a deployed system is safe under all adversaries. We analyze Daikaiju AIOps as a case study, distinguishing its installable Kaiju Spine/FASHSD substrate from separately deployed or manually installed incident-loop and agent-wiring reference components. Source inspection identifies signed incident and ticket paths, exact allowlist matching, a checkpoint-based autonomy gate, tool hooks, grounding filters, and failure-mode tests. These provide concrete mechanisms for reducing error propagation, but the repository does not establish a statistically characterized end-to-end field safety rate or universal resistance to compromised hosts, keys, models, or operators. The contribution is a testable safety case: **independence of routine execution is defensible only when independent policy enforcement, scoped authority, and empirical assurance jointly support it.**

**Keywords:** agentic AI, bounded autonomy, AIOps, defense in depth, human oversight, fail-closed execution, safety case.

## 1. Introduction: the strongest case against independent agents

The objection that “AI cannot be independent” is not merely that models hallucinate. Its strongest form is that an agent interpreting untrusted observations, choosing tools, and modifying infrastructure can compound errors faster than a person can detect them. Prompt injection may turn observed content into instructions; a plausible diagnosis may be false; an apparently benign repair may destroy data; compromised credentials may turn a small agent into a privileged operator; and logs or approval screens can themselves mislead. Worse, correlated failure of model, tools, and oversight defeats multiple controls at once. Under this view, *human approval for consequential changes* is not an inconvenience but a necessary boundary. OWASP's treatment of excessive agency and prompt injection gives this concern a concrete security basis [2]. Human-factors research likewise warns that changing the level of automation changes the operator's task rather than simply eliminating it [3].

We accept the objection for unrestricted autonomy. We reject its universal extension to every bounded action: a deterministic watchdog that restarts a known service on a verified signature is already an autonomous actor, although not an unrestricted reasoning agent. The research question is therefore **which actions, under which preconditions, with which independent checks, can be delegated?** Daikaiju explicitly separates a self-healing substrate from planning, reasoning, and execution roles, and marks its agent integration as outside the default installer (`README.md`; `incident-loop/README.md`; `agent-wiring/README.md`).

### 1.1 Claim and operational definition

Let an action request be `a`, its originating evidence `e`, system state `s`, and current authorization state `L`. A *bounded autonomous action* is permitted only if:

`Permit(a,e,s,L) = Authenticated(e) ∧ Fresh(e) ∧ InScope(a,s) ∧ Allowlisted(a,s) ∧ WithinBudget(a,s) ∧ Authorized(L,a) ∧ Verifiable(a)`.

This predicate is a **normative synthesis**, not a claim that one repository function implements every conjunct. In particular, authentication, freshness, scope, and verification have to be checked at their respective trust boundaries, and any missing check defeats the proposed assurance argument. The model may propose an action; the policy boundary must determine whether it can happen. The hypothesis is testable: under fault injection and adversarial inputs, unauthorized actions should be refused even when the model recommends them, and permission should fall back to supervision when a qualifying failure occurs.

## 2. Related work

**AI governance and agent security.** NIST's AI Risk Management Framework organizes governance, mapping, measurement, and management rather than equating deployment with proof of safety [1]. OWASP's LLM risk taxonomy (2025 edition, since updated) highlights prompt injection, improper output handling, and excessive agency [2]. These motivate controls outside the model's prompt and tests at the tool boundary. ReAct demonstrates an architecture that interleaves language-model reasoning and tool actions, but its task results are not a guarantee of safe infrastructure execution [4].

**AIOps and self-healing.** Google's SRE account of automation distinguishes increasingly integrated forms of automation and cautions that external automation can become stale as the system evolves [5]. Daikaiju's watchdog-first design belongs to this lineage: a fixed repair for a known failure is qualitatively different from granting a model general shell authority (`kaiju-gais-package/fashsd/README.md`; `kaiju-gais-package/fashsd/fm01_container_exited.sh`; `incident-loop/watchdog/fm12_missing_image_snapshot.sh`).

**Human-in-the-loop autonomy.** Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens distinguish automation of information acquisition, analysis, decision selection, and implementation, with different levels appropriate to each [3]. This is a better conceptual model than a binary “autonomous/not autonomous” label. Daikaiju's checkpoint and review mechanisms illustrate the idea of varying *implementation authority* without necessarily preventing autonomous monitoring or diagnosis (`agent-wiring/mokaima-adapter/checkpoint_gate.py`; `agent-wiring/mokaima-adapter/kaiju_mokaima_step5_review.py`).

**Guardrail tooling.** Daikaiju includes application-level filters for citation grounding, reference existence, incident fabrication, and enforcement, and execution-side hooks for pre-tool validation, command fidelity, budgets, sandbox evidence, and audit logging (`bakaidah/filters/`; `angkai/hooks/`). These are repository-specific implementations of a broader principle: a model's answer is not sufficient evidence that a proposed tool invocation is safe. They should not be mistaken for a formal security proof.

## 3. Case-study system design and deployment boundary

The publicly described system has three functional layers. **Kaiju GAIS** supplies a bootstrap, portable guardrail guidance, a version-controlled memory vault, and FASHSD self-healing checks. **Kaiju Spine** is the installer/distribution layer: `install.sh` prepares the vault under `~/.kaiju-spine`, configuration under `/etc/kaiju-spine/`, and watchdog scripts under `/opt/kaiju-spine/watchdog/` (`README.md`; `install.sh`; `MABS_Standalone.md`). **The agent and incident layers** provide a more ambitious closed loop, but `incident-loop/` and `agent-wiring/` are explicitly not installed by `install.sh` (`README.md`; both subsystem READMEs).

The named roles are **Mokaima** (planning/coordination), **Bakaidah** (read-only reasoning and structured diagnosis), and **Angkai** (execution subject to hooks and policy). The adapter, A2A server/executor, and gateway patch illustrate agent communication; the upstream gateway is not bundled (`agent-wiring/mokaima-adapter/kaiju_mokaima_a2a_adapter.py`; `agent-wiring/bakaidah-a2a/bakaidah_a2a_server.py`; `agent-wiring/bakaidah-a2a/bakaidah_executor.py`; `agent-wiring/gateway/NOTICE.md`). The relay is another separately installable edit-brief interface (`relay/README_relay_mcp.md`; `relay/install_relay_mcp.sh`).

The incident reference path is: watchdog detection → local known-fix attempt and bounded retry → signed publication → intake → read-only diagnosis → structured response and allowlist check → review and checkpoint decision → signed ticket → SSH forced command/ticket runner → one authorized repair → result handling (`incident-loop/README.md`; `incident-loop/watchdog/fashsd_incident_lib.sh`; `incident-loop/watchdog/kaiju_incident_publish.sh`; `incident-loop/intake/kaiju_incident_intake.py`; `incident-loop/orchestrator/kaiju_incident_orchestrator.py`; `incident-loop/watchdog/kaiju_ticket_runner.py`). This is an **architectural path**, not evidence that every public checkout runs it automatically.

## 4. Guardrail architecture: defense in depth

| Boundary / failure mode | Visible control | What it can support, and what it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|
| Uncertain local health observations | FASHSD failure-mode scripts and watchdog configuration (`kaiju-gais-package/fashsd/fm01_container_exited.sh`, `fm02_daemon_down.sh`, `fm07_dns_failure.sh`, `fashsd_config_lib.sh`) | Deterministic, condition-specific checks; not correctness for unknown failures. |
| Repeated or flapping repairs | Attempt cap and flap guard (`incident-loop/watchdog/fashsd_incident_lib.sh`) | Limits a known failure's local retry behavior; not all possible denial-of-service patterns. |
| Untrusted incident transport | Publisher, intake, signature checks (`incident-loop/watchdog/kaiju_incident_publish.sh`; `incident-loop/intake/kaiju_incident_intake.py`) | Reduces unauthorized incident injection under key-security assumptions; does not survive a stolen signing key. |
| Model-produced diagnoses and repair names | Structured task schema, citation/reference/grounding filters, service-specific exact allowlist (`agent-wiring/mokaima-adapter/kaiju_a2a_task_schema.py`; `bakaidah/filters/bakaidah_citation_grounding_filter.py`; `bakaidah/filters/bakaidah_reference_existence_filter.py`; `incident-loop/orchestrator/kaiju_incident_orchestrator.py`) | Separates suggestion from executable authority; filter bypass and poisoned evidence remain threats. |
| Unauthorized escalation of action | Review, scope gate, checkpoint state (`agent-wiring/mokaima-adapter/kaiju_mokaima_step5_review.py`; `autonomy_scope_gate.py`; `checkpoint_gate.py`) | Conditional authorization; effectiveness depends on correct configuration and enforcement on every action path. |
| Dangerous tool invocations | Pre-tool chain, action budget, command fidelity, sandbox proof, production signatures (`angkai/hooks/pretooluse_chain.sh`; `action_budget_pretooluse.sh`; `command_fidelity_pretooluse.sh`; `sandbox_proof_gate.py`; `production_matcher.py`) | Layered restrictions on supported hook paths; not a universal sandbox or protection from bypassing the hook runtime. |
| Unauthorized repair execution | Signed ticket and ticket runner, script allowlist (`incident-loop/orchestrator/kaiju_incident_orchestrator.py`; `incident-loop/watchdog/kaiju_ticket_runner.py`) | Restricts the intended remote execution path; trust still depends on runner host, key custody, and script integrity. |
| Post-action accountability and recovery | Audit hook, ledger/stop validation, rollback-related hooks (`angkai/hooks/audit_log_posttooluse.sh`; `ledger_stophook.py`; `stop_hook_validator.py`; `kai086_auto_rollback_gate.py`) | Evidence and recovery mechanisms on configured paths; not proof of tamper-proof logging or successful rollback in all cases. |

The key architectural distinction is **independent enforcement**: prompts and MABS policy prose specify expected conduct, whereas filters, gates, ticket validation, and tool hooks can reject actions at execution boundaries (`MABS_Standalone.md`; `bakaidah/filters/`; `angkai/hooks/`; `incident-loop/watchdog/kaiju_ticket_runner.py`). Independence is imperfect where controls share the same machine, administrator, secrets, or mutable configuration. Defense in depth must be evaluated against *common-mode compromise*, not simply by counting checks.

## 5. An autonomy ladder: earned, scoped, and revoked

A useful interpretation of the repository's checkpoint machinery is a **proposed assurance ladder**, not a claim that the entire stack implements a single globally synchronized state machine:

1. **Observe:** collect and classify incidents without infrastructure-changing model actions.
2. **Recommend:** draft a diagnosis and proposed repair; a human decides whether to execute.
3. **Supervised execution:** perform scoped changes after explicit review, with evidence and verification.
4. **Bounded autonomous execution:** issue a ticket without per-incident approval only when the current checkpoint state permits it and the service/script pair is exactly allowlisted.
5. **Revocation:** on a detected violation, failed verification, drift, or missing assurance evidence, return to a supervised state and require fresh qualifying evidence before re-promotion.

This is a *research operationalization* informed by `agent-wiring/mokaima-adapter/checkpoint_gate.py`, `checkpoint_ntfy.py`, `autonomy_scope_gate.py`, `kaiju_mokaima_step5_review.py`, and `incident-loop/orchestrator/kaiju_incident_orchestrator.py`. The orchestrator explicitly requires `checkpoint_state == "fully_autonomous"` before ticket issuance, and reports a held state otherwise (`incident-loop/orchestrator/kaiju_incident_orchestrator.py`). The project's status page reports a previous re-promotion after three supervised qualifying rounds, but the repository alone does not independently verify all runtime state transitions or establish an optimal statistical promotion threshold (`docs-site/docs/status.md`).

Promotion should be **earned per action class and environment**, using passing negative tests, signed evidence, clean supervised runs, stable target configuration, and explicit human ownership. Revocation should be automatic for an observed breach and possible manually at any time. A “fully autonomous” *checkpoint label* is not the same as unrestricted operational authority: it is still subject to allowlists, ticket checks, and other gates (`incident-loop/orchestrator/kaiju_incident_orchestrator.py`; `incident-loop/watchdog/kaiju_ticket_runner.py`).

## 6. Evaluation: observed evidence versus unproven claims

**Method.** We inspected all 174 files in the supplied archive, including scripts, tests, documentation, configuration examples, systemd units, HTML/JSON diagrams, and gateway patch. The assessment is **static code-and-document review**; it does not assert that we executed the project's test suites or reproduced its deployments. Statements below distinguish test *presence*, repository-reported results, and independent field measurements.

**Present evidence.** The repository includes a large shell test harness for FASHSD, configuration fixtures, a mocked fm12 repair test, Python tests for agent wiring, broker and relay, and an Angkai guardrail regression harness (`kaiju-gais-package/fashsd/tests/run_tests.sh`; `kaiju-gais-package/fashsd/tests/TESTING.md`; `incident-loop/tests/test_fm12_mocked.sh`; `agent-wiring/tests/`; `angkai/broker/tests/test_kaiju_broker_v3.py`; `angkai/tests/angkai_guardrail_regression.sh`; `relay/tests/`). Documentation reports clean-VM installer runs on several distributions, a 395-second Ubuntu 24.04 full-path run with retained log, and forced-live fm12 exercises (`README.md`; `docs-site/docs/status.md`; `docs/Regression_Results.md`). These are *reported observations* with varying artifact completeness, not independently replicated results in this paper.

**Re-run test evidence (update, commit `8179350`).** After the static review, every offline suite in the repository was made runnable from a fresh checkout and is now run by CI on each push (`tests/run_all.sh`; `.github/workflows/tests.yml`): 12 suites, all passing. They include the ticket runner end to end with real locking and subprocesses (79 cases, among them corrupt stored results, invalid health-poll configuration, failed script-copy cleanup and duplicate-ticket claims), offline guardrail tests for the runner, scope gate and checkpoint gate, and the broker, relay and Mokaima adapter suites. Two deliberate mutations of the runner (signature check forced true, pin check disabled) were each caught by a failing test. All of this is mocked or local: it shows the checks behave as coded, not how often they fire in the field. The Angkai hooks, Bakaidah filters and `install.sh` are not yet covered by these suites (`tests/README.md`).

**Mechanism-level evidence.** The orchestrator's service-specific allowlist lookup and hold condition are inspectable in code; the runner and publisher provide separately inspectable message/command checks (`incident-loop/orchestrator/kaiju_incident_orchestrator.py`; `incident-loop/watchdog/kaiju_ticket_runner.py`; `incident-loop/watchdog/kaiju_incident_publish.sh`). This supports a narrower statement: **the reference implementation contains concrete barriers to certain classes of unauthorized action.** It does not support a claim that every deployed path is configured correctly, that all bypasses are excluded, or that AI-generated diagnosis is reliably correct.

**Absent evidence.** No repository-wide, independently audited dataset establishes incident denominators, false-positive/false-negative rates, unauthorized-action frequency, mean time to recovery across representative deployments, rollback success, compromise resilience, or confidence intervals. The status page itself says that a live capture of the four-attempt cap firing is pending, and that Check Alerts' report script is not published (`docs-site/docs/status.md`; `README.md`). A rigorous field study would need immutable incident traces, predeclared failure classes, adverse-input and key-compromise trials, operator-approval baselines, blind adjudication of diagnoses, time-to-repair distributions, and reproducible release/configuration snapshots.

**Falsification protocol.** For each gate, mutate or bypass one predicate in a controlled disposable environment; inject a stale/replayed/forged incident, out-of-scope command, ambiguous allowlist entry, malformed model reply, compromised evidence source, or failed verification; assert that the *effect* is denied, not merely that a warning appears. Run the same cases at every autonomy level and across fresh installs. Treat a single unauthorized infrastructure-changing action as a counterexample to the proposed envelope, while distinguishing safety failures from liveness failures.

## 7. Threats to validity

**Construct validity:** “safe” has no single universal measure; this paper defines it narrowly as prevention of unauthorized actions under stated assumptions. **Internal validity:** code checks can be bypassed through deployment mistakes, shared privileges, unexpected invocation paths, or compromised dependencies. **External validity:** a homelab/small-infrastructure configuration is not evidence for multi-tenant enterprise or critical-infrastructure use (`README.md`; `Hardware_Compatibility_Matrix.md`). **Measurement validity:** documentation-reported test success is not equivalent to independently rerun tests, and a passing mocked test does not estimate live failure probability (`docs-site/docs/status.md`; `kaiju-gais-package/fashsd/tests/TESTING.md`). **Temporal validity:** behavior depends on the reviewed commit, external gateway and model versions, and deployed configurations (`agent-wiring/gateway/NOTICE.md`; `agent-wiring/mcpo/docker-compose.yml`). **Selection bias:** the documented induced failures need not resemble future failures or malicious inputs.

## 8. Limitations and strongest rebuttal

The strongest rebuttal is that even a signed, allowlisted repair can be unsafe: an attacker may poison the original health evidence, steal the signing key, compromise the allowlisted script, exploit the runner's privileges, or trigger a correct repair at the wrong time. A human reviewer can sometimes recognize contextual harm that a mechanical allowlist cannot. This is correct. The answer is **not** that layering eliminates the problem; it is that the autonomy claim must shrink to the threat model actually supported by independent checks, key isolation, operational telemetry, and tests. For high-impact, irreversible, unfamiliar, or cross-boundary actions, the appropriate authorization level remains human-controlled.

Additional limits are substantial. Agent wiring and incident loop are not in the default installer (`README.md`; `agent-wiring/README.md`; `incident-loop/README.md`). The upstream A2A gateway is external (`agent-wiring/gateway/NOTICE.md`). Some status claims concern runtime installations whose full state is not reconstructible from this archive (`docs-site/docs/status.md`). Prompts and filters can reduce unsupported output but cannot certify semantic truth (`bakaidah/bakaidah_system_prompt_v3.10.txt`; `bakaidah/filters/bakaidah_grounded_drafting_filter.py`). Hooks are not a substitute for operating-system privilege separation (`angkai/hooks/`). Consequently this paper does **not** claim a formal proof, zero hallucinations, comprehensive adversarial resistance, or a measured reduction in incident harm.

## 9. Future work

The most valuable next step is a reproducible end-to-end disposable testbed that installs the same components as the documented operational path, pins dependencies, records signed traces, and exercises every refusal branch (`install.sh`; `incident-loop/`; `agent-wiring/`). Next, conduct controlled red-team evaluations of injection through incident descriptions and retrieved references, key rotation/replay, compromised allowlist/script, SSH boundary errors, and hook bypass (`bakaidah/filters/`; `incident-loop/`; `angkai/hooks/`). Add an explicit per-service policy manifest with action impact, preconditions, postconditions, expiration, and automatic downgrade rules (`agent-wiring/mokaima-adapter/autonomy_scope_gate.py`; `checkpoint_gate.py`). Finally, report longitudinal, denominator-based incident outcomes and compare supervised with bounded-autonomous modes under matched failure distributions. These are proposed experiments, **not completed results**.

## 10. Conclusion

“An AI agent cannot be independent” is compelling when independence means unbounded authority based on model judgment. It is too broad when independence means executing **specific, preauthorized, verifiable actions** under externally enforced constraints. Daikaiju AIOps demonstrates an intelligible *design direction*: deterministic self-healing, authenticated incident handoffs, grounded reasoning interfaces, exact allowlists, checkpoint-controlled release, execution hooks, and reviewable evidence (`kaiju-gais-package/fashsd/`; `incident-loop/`; `agent-wiring/`; `bakaidah/filters/`; `angkai/hooks/`). Its public repository does **not** yet establish comprehensive field safety. The defensible conclusion is conditional: **autonomy is earned by evidence, limited by policy, and revoked on failure; the model's confidence is never the authorization boundary.**
